What an AI consultant actually does for a small business

By Fernando Rojas, Cofounder of Prime AI Consultants

November 26, 2026

Most small businesses do not have an AI problem. They have a specific problem that is costing them money, and AI is sometimes part of fixing it.

So what does an AI consultant actually do? At Prime AI Consultants, the job is a sequence of steps, and building anything comes late in it. It starts with a conversation about how a business runs. Then a closer look at the part of the business that conversation points to. Then a decision about what is worth fixing, and in what order. Only then is anything built, and the work ends with teaching the people who will run it. This post walks through those steps in order, for the businesses we work with in Santa Clarita and across Southern California.

It starts with your business, not with a tool

The first step is a conversation about how the business actually runs. Where do customers come in? Where does the work slow down? Which problems are costing money, and which ones are only annoying?

Nobody can learn a business from its website. That is why the first step is a conversation, not a sales pitch. What a business needs gets worked out with the people who run it, in their own words, before anyone names a tool.

At Prime, this conversation is free, and Fernando runs it on every engagement. What comes out of it is an agreement on what is worth looking at first, before anything is built or priced. Tools are chosen last here, not first.

Finding where the money is actually going

Next comes a closer look, and it has a different name depending on the business. For a local business, it is an audit of how the business shows up and gets reached: the Google profile and its reviews, search and AI visibility, the website, and what happens after an inquiry comes in. For a law firm or a manufacturer, it is a diagnostic, a closer look at one part of how the business runs.

The point of looking is to find out, not to confirm a guess made beforehand. Whatever it turns up comes from the business itself: its own listings, its own numbers, its own way of handling the work. Sometimes it confirms an area is in good shape. Sometimes it shows one problem clearly worth fixing first.

The findings are delivered in writing, and they belong to the business whether or not any build follows. The goal is a clear picture of what is actually happening, instead of a long list of everything AI could theoretically do.

Deciding what is worth fixing, and in what order

Not every problem found is worth fixing, and not every fix needs AI. Part of the job is saying so, out loud, before any money moves.

At Prime the order is simple. Start with whatever the audit or diagnostic shows is costing the most. Start small, in one place. Prove it works there before it goes any wider.

What already works stays untouched. New work is added to it, and the systems a business already relies on stay in place, and the business keeps running on them. And if a build is not worth doing, the consultant should say that in this step, not after a contract is signed.

Building the fix inside what you already use

Only now does anything get built. The build is the agreed fix, made for that business and owned by it. Depending on the problem, that can mean a website that explains the business clearly, a search presence that helps the right people find it, systems connected so information lives in one place, a small custom tool, or AI placed in one specific spot, such as help with follow up.

AI goes where it genuinely helps, and only where it makes the business money. It is a tool in the build, not the product being sold.

Where AI touches customers, it starts carefully: a narrow job first, often after hours or in shadow mode, where it runs without speaking for the business while a person reviews what it does, with clear limits and a way to turn it off. What gets built is documented in plain language the business can follow.

Teaching your team to run it

This is the step that is easiest to skip, and at Prime it is a named part of the process: teach and hand over. It is step four, and the work is not finished without it.

Training happens in person, in plain language, for the people who will actually use what was built. Written guidance is left behind, so the knowledge does not live in one person's memory.

The test of a finished job is an ordinary day. The work runs on a regular Tuesday, with the regular team, without the consultant in the room. Ownership is real only when the business can run what it owns, not just when the paperwork says it is theirs.

What an AI consultant should never do

A few lines should not be crossed, by us or by anyone you hire for this kind of work.

Never sell a blanket AI system before understanding the business. It can upset customers who expected a person, run up monthly costs, and lock the business to one provider.

Never quote a firm price before understanding the work. Pricing should come after the first conversation, as a written estimate you can say no to.

Never replace the system the business runs on as a first move, and never make the business dependent. If the work stops working the day the consultant leaves, it was not finished.

And never hide behind vague words without saying, in plain terms, what will actually be built and who will run it.

Questions owners ask

Do I need an AI consultant, or do I just need a website?

Sometimes a website is the whole answer. If customers cannot find you, cannot understand what you do, or cannot reach you, that is a website and search problem first, and AI may not belong in it at all. A good first conversation should tell you which problem you actually have before anyone suggests a build.

What does an AI consultant cost for a small business?

It depends on the business and the problem, which is why Prime does not publish package prices. The first conversation is free. After it, you get a written estimate based on your business and what it needs, and you can say no to it. Anyone quoting a firm price before that conversation is pricing a package, not your problem. (A later post in this series covers cost in full.)

Will you teach us to run what you build, or do we have to keep calling you?

Teaching is part of the work. We train the people who will use what was built, in person and in plain language, and leave written guidance behind. Some businesses choose to keep working with us after handover, and that is their choice. Nothing we build should need us in the room to keep working.
