What is operational context, and why does AI need it before a pilot?

By Fernando Rojas and Antonio Rojas, Cofounders of Prime AI Consultants

November 26, 2026

General Manufacturing Operations · 8 min read

Direct answer

Operational context is the written record of how your plant actually runs: how a term like scrap or yield is defined, which system owns a decision, who validates the answer, and what the floor does when the plan changes. AI needs that record before a pilot, because without it a model learns your reports rather than your operation, and gives confident answers built on definitions your own teams do not share.

Who this is for: VP Operations, VP Manufacturing, or CTO at a one to five plant manufacturer considering a first AI pilot across MES, ERP, and floor systems.

The problem shows up in one word

Ask three people on the same floor what the scrap rate was yesterday and you can get three correct answers that do not agree, because each is counting something slightly different while using the same word. The disagreement is rarely carelessness: it is what happens when a definition lives in habit instead of in a shared record everyone can point to and challenge.

Wang and Strong made the research version of this point long ago: data quality cannot be judged on accuracy alone. Fitness for use, including whether data is represented consistently and in context, is what determines whether a decision maker can rely on it.[1] A number can be clean in a system and still be unusable for a decision if the people using it mean different things by it.

Illustrative example: composite, not a named client

Take Line 4, a production line at a two plant injection molder. The team asks for a model that predicts scrap rate by shift so supervisors can act earlier. On paper the data looks ready. Scrap is recorded in three places: at the press when a part is rejected, at inspection when a lot is sampled, and at end of shift when the supervisor closes the count. Startup shots after a mold change are sometimes counted as scrap and sometimes logged separately. Regrind that goes back into the process is handled one way on days and another way on nights, and rework that is later accepted is sometimes removed from the scrap total and sometimes left in. None of this is hidden. Everyone on the floor knows their part of it. It has never been written down in one place, so a model trained on the combined history learns an average of three different operations and calls it one number.

That is the pattern Prime AI Consultants sees most often: the pilot does not fail because the model is weak, but because the operation underneath it was never described clearly enough to hold anyone, human or model, to the same definition.

Reports are not the operation

Most plants already have more reporting than they can use, and an overnight export can disagree with the MES screen without either being broken. MES records what happened on the floor; finance records what can be billed, scrapped, or closed; a supervisor’s spreadsheet often holds the exception logic that makes the day workable. Treat those sources as interchangeable, and an AI pilot inherits the argument instead of resolving it.

Christoph Gröger, enterprise architect for data analytics at Bosch, makes a related point in “There is no AI without data,” his 2021 article in Communications of the ACM: industrial AI depends on data that is integrated, contextualized, and understood across systems rather than simply collected.[2] The value is not in having more data. It is in knowing what the data refers to, where it came from, and which decision it is allowed to govern.

Manufacturing sites raise the stakes, because a yield or lot definition that shifts by team or by step can travel a long way before anyone notices, and the principle is the same in discrete and process plants. If the definition moves, every answer built on it moves with it.

Discover, then Define

Prime AI Consultants starts in two steps before any build is discussed. The order matters, because you cannot define what you have not first observed as it actually happens.

01

Discover

Work with the people who run the floor to map how systems really behave today: where a number is created, where it changes hands, and where the workaround lives. The goal is an honest picture, not an ideal process diagram.

02

Define

Write that picture down in plain language for one system and one decision at a time: the definition, the owner, the source, and the exception. For one decision area, this is days to a few weeks of focused work, not a months long documentation project.

Only after that record exists does a pilot have something stable to learn from and be judged against. Governance keeps it current as tools, products, and people change. Activation is where a model is finally introduced, inside boundaries operations already understands.

Trust is decided on the floor

Even a technically sound pilot can lose the floor quickly if the first visible answers are wrong in ways operators recognize immediately. Dietvorst, Simmons, and Massey found that people who see an algorithm make a mistake become less willing to rely on it afterward, even when it performs well on average.[3] That finding comes from forecasting tasks, not manufacturing, so it should not be overstated. But it matches what plant leaders describe: once supervisors decide an answer cannot be trusted, they stop checking it, work around it, and the pilot loses its chance to improve.

Operational context protects against that early loss of trust, because validation can be specific: a named owner can say whether an answer is right for this definition, on this line, under these conditions.

The operational context checklist

For one system and one decision, write these eight items down before a pilot is approved. If any item cannot be answered, that gap is the first work to do.

  1. The decision. The single decision this pilot will support, stated in one sentence a supervisor would recognize.
  2. The definition. What the key term means for this decision, in plain words, including what is counted and what is not.
  3. The source of truth for this decision. Which system owns this decision. There is no single source of truth for a whole plant, only a source per decision.
  4. The handoffs. Where the information changes hands, changes format, or changes meaning between creation and use.
  5. The exceptions the floor already knows. Startup, changeover, rework, regrind, and the other cases that do not follow the standard path, written as the floor describes them.
  6. The owner and the validator. Who owns the decision today by role, and who will validate AI answers for that same decision.
  7. Reports that disagree, and why. Where two reports give different numbers for the same period, and the timing or definition rule that explains the difference.
  8. The validation set and the update rule. A small set of past cases with agreed answers to test against, plus who updates this record and how quickly when the floor, the product, or the system changes.

What not to do

Do not start with a platform purchase and work backwards to find a problem it can solve.

Do not try to document the entire plant before starting. One system and one decision is enough to prove the approach.

Do not average away a disagreement between systems. Explain it, name which source governs which decision, and keep the gap visible.

Do not let a vendor validate its own answers for your operation. A vendor can test a model, only your operation can validate a decision.

Do not widen the pilot to satisfy every stakeholder at once. A scope that pleases everyone in the meeting is usually too wide to prove anything on the floor.

Frequently asked questions

Do we need a data warehouse before we can do this?

No. You need understood data for one scope, not a new platform for all of it. The checklist can be completed with the systems you already have, and it often shows that a warehouse is not the first investment to make.

Do we need to document everything first?

No. Start with one system and one decision that matters this quarter. A complete record for a narrow scope is more valuable than a partial record for the whole plant.

Who should write the operational context down?

The people who do the work, with Prime facilitating. IT is essential for systems knowledge, but definitions that only IT writes rarely match how the floor actually runs.

How long does Discover and Define take?

Days to a few weeks for one decision area, depending on how many shifts and systems are involved. The work is interviews, tracing real cases through systems, and agreeing definitions, not a long reporting project.

What happens if we skip this and go straight to a pilot?

The pilot may still produce answers, but no one will agree on whether they are right, because the definition and the validator were never settled. That is where most early pilots lose support.
