Know Your OperationBefore You Trust AI.

Write the operation down first

Ops and finance define yield differently, and the export can disagree with the MES screen. With the floor team, we document how the operation runs, in a record you can challenge.

What is usually wrong

The model isn’t the first problem.

In most plants the data is already there. What it means is harder to find. Reports do not agree, the logic inside the applications has no clear owner, spreadsheets have filled the gaps, and the knowledge that ties it together still sits with the people on the floor.

  1. 01

    Different sources

    Operations trusts the MES screen. Finance trusts the export that ran last night. No one designed the two to agree, so the same question can give two different answers before any new tool is involved.

  2. 02

    Different meanings

    Ask how yield is calculated and you may get two answers, sometimes three, each one sounding right. That is why the definition belongs in writing, in one place anyone can check.

  3. 03

    Different answers

    Connect a model before that writing happens and the confusion does not go away. It reaches more people, faster. We treat the written record as the first step, not something to sort out afterward.

AI for MES

Your MES holds the operational record. Engineers shouldn’t need raw SQL to question it.

Manufacturing databases were built to run MES applications, not to answer questions from an AI tool. The logic behind their numbers is spread across procedures, overnight jobs, reports that do not match, and people who have been on the floor for years. We recover that context before anyone connects a model to anything your production depends on.

The failure pattern

Pointing AI at raw MES tables gives answers the floor cannot trust.

Yield comes out wrong, WIP counts disagree with what is actually on the line, and a lot reads as clear when everyone on the floor knows it sits on hold for a reason the table never carries.

Querying the database is not the milestone. Answering a real floor question from logic your team has written and approved is. If that logic is not written yet, we pause and write it first.

Schema alone is not meaning

Tables, views, procedures, scheduled jobs, and report logic all shape what a number means. The database structure is only the starting point.

Hidden relationships are common

Many MES databases record few formal links between tables. The real relationships live in naming habits, in procedures, and in what the screens show people every day.

Governed access or no access

We propose read only paths through approved views and review by your team, not unrestricted access to the underlying tables.

The failure pattern

A system of record can still give three versions of the same number.

A model will not settle which version counts. That call comes first: which source is authoritative for this decision, how the number is calculated, and who owns the call when sites disagree.

Meaning is scattered

Configurations, customizations, reports, and local practice all shape what a number means. No single ERP screen tells the whole story.

Definitions diverge

Different sites, teams, and units often use the same field to mean slightly different things. The differences stay invisible until someone answers a question across all of them.

Access needs review

Privacy, security, legal, and finance may all need to weigh in before a pilot gets access to ERP data. That review belongs at the start.

What is operational context, and why does AI need it before a pilot?

Operational context is the written record of how a plant runs: which systems hold which facts, how numbers are calculated, what the floor terms mean, who owns each decision, and where the known exceptions live. AI needs it before a pilot because a model reasons only from what it is given, and most of that record was never written down.

By Fernando Rojas, reviewed by Antonio Rojas · About 4 minutes

Who this is for: leaders at manufacturers with one to five plants who are weighing a first AI pilot and want to know what has to be true before it starts.

The problem, in plain language

Most first pilots are scoped around a model and a data source, assuming that once the two are connected the answers will follow. The model is rarely the first problem. The harder question is whether anyone has written down what the data means where it was created.

A plant’s systems record transactions, not the reasoning that makes them readable. The Manufacturing Execution System (MES) knows a lot was placed on hold. Why that hold code covers two situations on two lines lives with a supervisor of fifteen years. The Enterprise Resource Planning system (ERP) carries a standard cost. The adjustment finance applies at month end sits in a spreadsheet only one analyst opens. Connect an AI tool to those systems and it inherits every gap without knowing the gaps exist.

So the pilot gives fluent, plausible answers until someone on the floor checks one against a number they know, the two disagree, and trust is gone. One visible error can cost the tool its credibility.

An illustrative floor example

The following scenario is illustrative. It is a composite written to show the pattern, and it does not describe any particular company or plant.

Consider a two plant molder that wants an assistant to answer a simple question: what was the scrap rate on Line 4 last week? The data is there and the calculation looks straightforward. Scrap divided by total production should give the answer in seconds.

An assistant pointed at the raw tables will calculate a scrap rate, and its arithmetic will be correct. The answer may still be wrong. Startup shots are marked as scrap, tucked into a code the MES counts one way and the plant spreadsheet counts another. Regrind sent back for a second shot is recorded as scrap on the second shift and not the first. A lot returned for rework is listed as good in one report and scrap in another. A rate that mixes those entries is not a fact about the line, although it looks like one.

The point is not that the data is bad. It is that the data needs a written account of how it came to be, and that account is what the pilot was missing from the start. Write the meaning down first. Then a pilot can be judged against a record people trust.
